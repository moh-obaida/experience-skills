# Deriving Identity: Brand World, Motifs, Competitors

Load when choosing a direction: where identity comes from and how to learn from others without copying.

Sections: Brand World · Product-Derived Motifs · Competitor Analysis Without Copying

## Brand World

A brand world is the coherent imaginary space a product's surfaces belong to. It gives
designers a way to make new surfaces feel related without copying old ones.

### Defining a world

Answer in a few sentences each:

1. **Setting:** if the product were a place, what place? (a well-lit workshop; a stadium at night;
   a quiet library; a lab bench; a sketchbook)
2. **Materials:** what is it made of? (paper, felt, metal, glass, light, ink, wood, pixels)
3. **Light:** where does light come from, and how does it behave? (soft daylight; stage spotlights;
   screen glow; none, flat)
4. **Geometry:** what shapes recur? (hexes, circles, grids, torn edges, hand-drawn lines)
5. **Energy:** calm, focused, energetic, playful, formal?
6. **Voice:** how does it talk?

### From world to decisions

| World element | Design decisions |
|---|---|
| Setting | Background treatment, imagery subject, layout mood |
| Materials | Surface treatment (texture, flat, layered), borders, shadows |
| Light | Gradients (as light, not decoration), shadows, highlights, dark vs light modes |
| Geometry | Motifs, corner treatments, icon style, pattern |
| Energy | Density, motion speed and easing, color saturation |
| Voice | Copy, naming, error messages |

### Example

A classroom trivia game:

- Setting: a game-show stage in a school gym.
- Materials: painted wood panels, bright vinyl, stage lights.
- Light: warm spotlights from above; the rest of the stage darker.
- Geometry: rounded rectangles like buzzer buttons; hex tiles on the board.
- Energy: high but readable at a distance.
- Voice: friendly host; short sentences; never sarcastic to kids.

Decisions: dark stage background with a warm radial light behind the task area; hex tile motif
in the background field; team colors bright and saturated; standard, large inputs; bold
condensed display type for scores; motion that lands like a buzzer (fast in, small overshoot).

### Keep it grounded

A world is a tool for consistency, not a license for theme-park decoration. Controls stay
usable; text stays legible; performance stays acceptable.

## Product-Derived Motifs

A motif is a recurring visual element that belongs to the product. Good motifs are found in
the product, not imported from a trend.

### Where to find motifs

| Source | Questions | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Core mechanic | What does the user do most? | Stacking blocks → stepped layouts; connecting dots → line motifs |
| Content artifacts | What objects does the product handle? | Tickets, cards, receipts, maps, charts, notes |
| Domain tools | What tools does the real-world domain use? | Rulers, grids, ledgers, stamps, tape, chalk |
| Game or board elements | Tiles, tokens, boards, cards, dice | Hex tiles, token circles |
| Data shapes | The shape of the data | Timelines, trees, networks, heatmaps |
| Cultural context | Where the product lives | Regional patterns, local typography traditions (with care and respect) |
| Name and story | The product's name or origin | A literal or abstract mark echoed in patterns |

### Turning a source into a motif

1. Abstract it: simplify to shape, line, or pattern.
2. Test at multiple scales: background field (large, low contrast), accent (small, high contrast).
3. Define where it appears: 3–5 recurring placements (environment backgrounds, headers,
   empty states, loading, results).
4. Define where it never appears: inside controls, over body text, in dense data regions.

### Intensity levels

- **Ambient:** low-contrast background pattern; environment only.
- **Structural:** corner cutouts, dividers, frames borrow the motif's geometry.
- **Featured:** motif at full strength in key moments (results, celebrations, hero).

Most surfaces should use ambient or structural. Featured is for moments.

### Anti-patterns

- Generic abstract blobs and waves (not derived from anything)
- Motifs so busy they reduce text contrast
- Motifs applied to every surface at the same intensity
- Cultural motifs used superficially or out of context

## Competitor Analysis Without Copying

Studying strong products is useful. Copying their surfaces is not.

### Method

1. **Pick the right comparisons:** direct competitors, adjacent products serving the same
   audience, and exemplary products from other domains that solve a similar problem.
2. **Observe specific decisions,** not vibes. Not "Linear feels premium" but "Linear uses dense
   rows, muted metadata, one strong status color, and instant keyboard response."
3. **Explain why each decision works for that product:** audience, frequency, density, brand.
4. **Check whether your product shares those conditions.** If not, the decision may not transfer.
5. **Translate the principle** into your product's world.
6. **Differentiate deliberately:** list what your product should do differently because its
   audience, mechanic, or values differ.

### Analysis template

```
Product: <name> · Surface: <which page/flow> · Observed: <date>
Decision: <specific, observable>
Why it works there: <audience, frequency, density, brand reason>
Conditions required: <what must be true for it to work>
Applies to us? <yes/partly/no, and why>
Translation: <how we would express the principle in our own world>
Do not copy: <surface specifics that are theirs>
```

### Legal and ethical boundaries

- Do not reuse proprietary assets, illustrations, copy, or distinctive trade dress.
- Link to public pages; describe in your own words.
- Do not scrape or redistribute screenshots without permission.

### Common copying failures

- Dark minimalist developer-tool aesthetic applied to a children's education product.
- Apple-style scroll storytelling on an internal admin tool.
- Gamification patterns from a language app applied to a finance product.
- Black-and-white grid minimalism without the typographic precision that made it work.
